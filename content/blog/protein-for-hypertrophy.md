# How much protein builds muscle?

Walk into any gym and you will hear protein advice ranging from "1 g per pound, always" to "you only need chicken twice a week." The gap between folklore and data is wide. FitTrack sets protein targets from peer-reviewed dose-response research so your daily macro goals reflect evidence, not gym myths.

This article unpacks **Morton et al. (2018)** — the meta-analysis behind FitTrack's protein recommendations — and shows how those numbers appear on your [nutrition dashboard](/nutrition).

## What Morton et al. studied

Morton RW, Murphy KT, McKellar SR, et al. (2018) published a systematic review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*. They pooled **49 randomized controlled trials** with **1,863 participants** who performed resistance training while varying dietary protein intake.

Key findings:

- Protein supplementation **significantly increased** fat-free mass gains (standardized mean difference 0.30; 95% CI 0.09 to 0.52) and leg press strength (SMD 0.27; 95% CI 0.05 to 0.49) versus control conditions.
- In meta-regression, the relationship between daily protein intake and fat-free mass accretion **plateaued near 1.62 g/kg/day** of body weight. Additional protein above that point did not produce meaningfully larger hypertrophy in the pooled data.
- Benefits were clearest when baseline protein was low; participants already eating high-protein diets saw smaller incremental gains from supplementation.

These results do not mean protein is unimportant. They mean there is an **evidence-based range** — enough to maximize hypertrophy for most lifters without forcing arbitrary excess.

## How this differs from deficit dieting

During caloric restriction, higher protein helps preserve lean mass. Helms ER, Aragon AA, Fitschen PJ (2014) reviewed protein needs for lean athletes in a deficit and recommended roughly **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass** — higher than Morton’s hypertrophy plateau because the goal shifts from building tissue to defending it.

FitTrack applies this distinction in code: muscle-building goals use Morton’s upper range, while fat-loss goals bias protein higher to protect lean mass (Helms et al., 2014).

## Practical takeaways

1. **Aim for at least ~1.6 g/kg/day** if hypertrophy is the goal; Morton et al. (2018) found diminishing returns beyond ~1.62 g/kg for most trainees.
2. **Go higher in a deficit** to protect muscle; Helms et al. (2014) support elevated protein when calories are restricted.
3. **Distribute protein across meals** — the meta-analysis included varied feeding patterns, but spreading intake supports daily totals without relying on a single shake.
4. **Track against a target, not a myth** — logging in FitTrack shows whether you are consistently inside the evidence-based band.
